    Register 5 · Substances of concern updated Sep 2026

    Teratogens register: which products can harm the unborn child, and who says so.

    Not on the list does not mean safe. The register holds substances of concern only, so a medicine or product missing from it may still harm a pregnancy. Do not stop a prescribed medicine because of this page: some, such as medicines for epilepsy, are dangerous to stop suddenly. It is not medical advice. Ask your doctor, your pharmacist or a teratology information service.

    Go to the register: 586 substances

    Families ask a simple question after a diagnosis: could something have caused this? No public authority answers it with one list. The World Health Organization keeps none. What exists is scattered across chemical law, medicines regulation and one American state. This register brings those lists together, names the source for every entry, states how strong the evidence is, and says where each substance is banned, restricted, labelled or simply allowed.

    For a question about a medicine or an exposure during a pregnancy, ask a teratology information service: CRAT in France, bumps in the United Kingdom, MotherToBaby in North America, or the ENTIS member in your country.

    01 · How to read it

    Three levels of evidence, seven sources, one status per jurisdiction.

    • Known: human evidence. In the EU this is category 1A of the harmonised classification; for medicines, a documented human teratogen; in California, a substance the State lists after its own experts review it, or because another law already requires the warning.
    • Presumed: strong animal evidence. Category 1B in the EU, a medicine contraindicated in pregnancy on animal data, or a substance California lists on an authoritative body’s review, which is usually a review of animal studies. California’s own wording is “known to the State”, a legal status rather than a statement about human evidence, so we read the basis of each listing rather than the phrase.
    • Suspected: limited evidence, category 2 in the EU, or an association shown in epidemiological studies.
    • How a pregnancy meets it: every entry is tagged with the ways a pregnant woman receives the substance at a dose that matters: Medicine, food and drink, tobacco and recreational drugs, home and personal care, air pollution, pesticides and biocides, work and industry. Each tag carries its own level of evidence, from the authority that speaks to that way of meeting it: a medicines regulator for treatment, a chemical classification for the substance handled at work or sold as a pesticide or in home products. A medicine can therefore be known as a treatment and presumed where it is made (27 entries). Where a source states the dose at which the effect is shown in people, the entry gives it, with the source (6 entries).
    • For pharmacists: for 71 medicines, among them all 24 that experts of the European Network of Teratology Information Services list as known human structural teratogens (Bluett-Duncan et al., 2025), the entry gives what a pharmacist needs to advise a pregnant woman: the regulator’s rules, when in pregnancy the harm occurs, the dose, the malformations and other effects, the absolute risk, what is required before conception and after stopping, what concerns the father, what to do after an exposure, and the evidence it all rests on. Each fact comes from a regulator’s product information (US FDA, EMA, ANSM, MHRA), a systematic review or a large registry study, and the details quote it word for word; two readers checked each one against its source. Where no such source states a fact, the entry leaves it out rather than guess. 9 of these medicines were on none of the lists above and enter the register on the experts’ list.
    • What the information services say: for 98 medicines, the entry links to the page that a teratology information service publishes on it: the CRAT in Paris, Embryotox in Berlin and the UK Teratology Information Service’s bumps leaflets. They are members of ENTIS; of its 31 centres, only a few publish a public page per medicine. Their terms allow a link, not a copy, so the register quotes nothing from them, and a link appears only when the page is about that medicine, not its class.
    • In cosmetics: for 30 entries used in cosmetics, the card gives what EU cosmetics law allows, the dose the safety assessment rests on, the effect on the unborn child it names, and the assessor’s conclusion, each quoted from the EU Cosmetics Regulation, an opinion of the EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety or a US Cosmetic Ingredient Review report.
    • A second authority: 205 entries also carry a classification made by the Japanese government, through the GHS classification projects of the National Institute of Technology and Evaluation, to implement the labelling and safety-data-sheet duties of the Industrial Safety and Health Act and the PRTR Law. 62 of them have no EU harmonised entry. Each classification names the ministry that made it and the fiscal year, and links to its own page with the studies it rests on. It corroborates an entry and adds a jurisdiction; it does not set the level, because a GHS hazard code does not separate category 1A from category 1B.
    • What was decided, and by whom: the paragraphs below describe what the law provides. A tag on a card describes what an authority actually decided about that substance, and names it. 334 entries carry at least one. The European Commission has refused 92 of them as pesticide active substances and approved 26; 190 may not be sold to the general public at all; 256 may not go into a cosmetic product; 24 need a Commission authorisation for any use; 7 are eliminated worldwide by treaty; and 27 have been banned or severely restricted by at least one country, which the card names. An approval is not a contradiction: the EU excludes a category 1A or 1B reproductive toxicant from pesticide approval unless exposure is negligible, and category 2 is not excluded at all. It is, though, a decision worth seeing next to the evidence.
    • How much is tolerable: for substances in the food chain, the European Food Safety Authority derives the intake it considers tolerable and names the effect that figure rests on. 82 entries carry such a value, read from EFSA’s pages and from its chemical hazards database, OpenFoodTox 3.0 (CC BY-ND 4.0). EFSA does not classify teratogens and its remit stops at food and feed, so its values sit beside the evidence level, never instead of it.

    Independent hazard frameworks. Manufacturers and certifiers increasingly rate chemicals with two non-profit frameworks that score developmental and reproductive toxicity among their endpoints. GreenScreen for Safer Chemicals (Clean Production Action) assigns Benchmarks 1 to 4, Benchmark 1 being a chemical of high concern, through assessments by licensed profilers such as ToxServices; its free List Translator flags as LT-1 any chemical that an authoritative list already classes as a high-hazard reproductive or developmental toxicant, and its public registry tells you, by CAS number, whether a full assessment exists. ChemFORWARD rates chemicals used in consumer products and building materials in hazard bands A to F across 24 endpoints; its published rating guidance places in band F, by list screening alone, every substance with an EU harmonised Repr. 1 classification or on the REACH candidate and authorisation lists as a reproductive toxicant. The two organisations reported in 2021 that their outputs are aligned. Full assessments sit behind subscriptions, so this register cannot import their scores; where an entry meets ChemFORWARD's published F-band criterion, it says so.

    The EU harmonised classification is binding law: once a substance carries a hazard statement for the unborn child (H360D, H361d and their variants), every container of it, and of mixtures containing it, must be labelled across the EU and EEA; categories 1A and 1B may not be sold to the general public, cannot be approved as pesticides and are banned from cosmetics. It says nothing about finished articles, food or medicines, which are outside its scope. Proposition 65 is binding in California only and requires a warning before exposure, not a ban; it is enforced through litigation. EMA decisions bind marketing authorisations across the EU: the medicine stays available, under a pregnancy prevention programme. WHO guidance binds no one. Our bibliography reports evidence, not law.

      Acenocoumarol

      CAS 152-72-7 · ATC B01AA07

      Also known or sold as INN acenocoumarol · France MINI-SINTROM, SINTROM · Italy SINTROM · Spain ACENOCUMAROL AUROVITAS, SINTROM · Other languages acénocoumarol (fr), Acenocumarol (de), acenocumarol (es), acenocumarolo (it), acenocumarol (pt), acenocoumarolum (la)

      Medicine · Known

      In pregnancyLimb defects named by the source

      Rules
      ANSM's December 2018 letter announced that vitamin K antagonists, including acenocoumarol, are contraindicated in pregnancy, except for women with a mechanical heart valve at high thromboembolic risk whose potential benefits outweigh the risks. source ↗
      When
      Malformations were described mostly after exposure between 6 and 9 weeks of pregnancy, counted from the first day of the last period (weeks of amenorrhoea). source ↗
      Dose
      A systematic review cites registry data that did not show reduced foetal loss with low first-trimester doses of vitamin K antagonists, low meaning 2 mg a day or less for acenocoumarol. source ↗
      Effects
      ENTIS experts list multiple malformations including an underdeveloped nose, stippled bone ends, skeletal and finger or toe (digital) defects, with reduced growth and facial differences. source ↗
      Other effects
      The French Sintrom label states that cerebral fetopathy, mainly central nervous system and eye anomalies, occurs in 1 to 2% of vitamin K antagonist exposures beyond 9 weeks of amenorrhoea. source ↗
      Risk
      The French label states that, with vitamin K antagonists including Sintrom, a malformation syndrome has been described in about 4% to 7% of exposed pregnancies, and gives no comparison rate. source ↗
      Before and after
      The French Sintrom label says women of childbearing age must be told to use effective contraception during treatment and until 3 days after stopping, and to plan pregnancies to allow a safer alternative. source ↗
      If exposed
      If Sintrom is used in pregnancy or pregnancy is discovered on treatment, the French label requires specialised prenatal diagnosis by ultrasound, or even MRI, adapted to the period of exposure. source ↗
      Evidence
      A multicentre prospective ENTIS cohort (1988–2004) followed 226 acenocoumarol-exposed pregnancies among 666 vitamin K antagonist exposures and 1,094 unexposed controls; its abstract reports results for the class as a whole. source ↗

      Acitretin

      CAS 55079-83-9 · ATC D05BB02

      Also known or sold as INN acitretin · US, discontinued Soriatane · France SORIATANE · Italy NEOTIGASON, ZORIAS · Spain ACITRETINA IFC, NEOTIGASON · Canada MINT-ACITRETIN, SORIATANE, TARO-ACITRETIN · Other languages acitrétine (fr), acitretina (es), acitretina (it), acitretine (nl), acitretina (pt)

      Medicine · Known

      In pregnancyLimb defects named by the source

      Rules
      France's product information strictly contraindicates acitretin in pregnant women and in women who can become pregnant unless every condition of the pregnancy prevention programme is met. source ↗
      When
      ANSM counts from treatment: a pregnancy that starts during acitretin treatment or within 3 years after stopping carries a very high risk of major malformations in the fetus. source ↗
      Dose
      The US label states that the blood-level threshold for increased birth defects has not been established for acitretin in humans, and post-treatment contraception time cannot be calculated precisely. source ↗
      Effects
      The US label lists reported major fetal abnormalities with acitretin or etretinate, including meningomyelocele, meningoencephalocele, multiple synostoses, facial dysmorphia, and limb or digit defects (syndactyly, absent terminal phalanges, hip, ankle and forearm malformations). source ↗
      Other effects
      Beyond malformations, acitretin's French product information states that there is also an increased risk of miscarriage. source ↗
      Before and after
      ANSM requires women who can become pregnant to use contraception from at least 1 month before acitretin, throughout treatment and for at least 3 years after stopping, even without periods. source ↗
      Father
      The US label says acitretin in semen appears to pose little, if any, risk to a fetus during or after a man's treatment, but the no-effect limit for teratogenicity is unknown. source ↗
      If exposed
      If a woman becomes pregnant on acitretin, ANSM requires stopping treatment and referring her to a physician specialised or experienced in teratology for evaluation and advice. source ↗
      Evidence
      Manufacturers' pharmacovigilance data assessed by EU regulators in 2018 counted 442 acitretin pregnancy cases since marketing, 132 conceived during treatment and 260 after it. source ↗

      Rules
      The US label contraindicates azathioprine for rheumatoid arthritis in pregnant women and says use in pregnancy should be avoided whenever possible, after careful weighing of risk and benefit. source ↗
      When
      ANSM says a newborn whose mother took azathioprine at the end of pregnancy may have anaemia, leukopenia, lymphopenia and/or thrombocytopenia. source ↗
      Dose
      ANSM says these newborn blood-count abnormalities are dose-dependent and usually transient, and that lowering the maternal dose when possible is desirable to prevent them. source ↗
      Effects
      A Swedish register study found an association between azathioprine exposure in early pregnancy and ventricular or atrial septal defects (adjusted OR 3.18, 95% CI 1.45-6.04). source ↗
      Other effects
      Health Canada says azathioprine can harm the fetus, including intra-uterine growth retardation, premature birth and low birth weight, particularly when combined with corticosteroids. source ↗

      In a French nationwide registry of mothers with IBD, major malformations occurred in 197.2 per 10,000 births exposed to thiopurines in the first trimester versus 175.2 per 10,000 unexposed births. source ↗

      The Jayempi (azathioprine) SmPC, authorised centrally in the EU, says male and female patients of reproductive age must use contraception during treatment and for at least six months after it ends. source ↗
      Father
      A 2023 meta-analysis of men with IBD found paternal thiopurine use was not associated with early pregnancy loss, preterm birth or congenital malformations (OR 1.07). source ↗
      If exposed
      ANSM says the exposed newborn should have a full blood count, and live attenuated vaccines should be deferred until after 6 months of age as a precaution. source ↗
