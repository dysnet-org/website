    Register 5 · Substances of concern updated Sep 2026

    Teratogens register: which products can harm the unborn child, and who says so.

    Go to the register: 586 substances

    Families ask a simple question after a diagnosis: could something have caused this? No public authority answers it with one list. The World Health Organization keeps none. What exists is scattered across chemical law, medicines regulation and one American state. This register brings those lists together, names the source for every entry, states how strong the evidence is, and says where each substance is banned, restricted, labelled or simply allowed.

    It is not medical advice. For a question about a medicine or an exposure during a pregnancy, ask a teratology information service: CRAT in France, bumps in the United Kingdom, MotherToBaby in North America, or the ENTIS member in your country.

    01 · How to read it

    Three levels of evidence, seven sources, one status per jurisdiction.

    • Known: human evidence. In the EU this is category 1A of the harmonised classification; for medicines, a documented human teratogen; in California, a substance the State lists after its own experts review it, or because another law already requires the warning.
    • Presumed: strong animal evidence. Category 1B in the EU, a medicine contraindicated in pregnancy on animal data, or a substance California lists on an authoritative body’s review, which is usually a review of animal studies. California’s own wording is “known to the State”, a legal status rather than a statement about human evidence, so we read the basis of each listing rather than the phrase.
    • Suspected: limited evidence, category 2 in the EU, or an association shown in epidemiological studies.
    • How a pregnancy meets it: every entry is tagged with the ways a pregnant woman receives the substance at a dose that matters: Medicine, food and drink, tobacco and recreational drugs, home and personal care, air pollution, pesticides and biocides, work and industry. Each tag carries its own level of evidence, from the authority that speaks to that way of meeting it: a medicines regulator for treatment, a chemical classification for the substance handled at work or sold as a pesticide or in home products. A medicine can therefore be known as a treatment and presumed where it is made (27 entries). Where a source states the dose at which the effect is shown in people, the entry gives it, with the source (11 entries).
    • Stage, dose and risk: for the 24 medicines that experts of the European Network of Teratology Information Services list as known human structural teratogens (Bluett-Duncan et al., 2025), the entry states when in pregnancy the harm occurs, the dose, the effects and the absolute risk. Each fact comes from a regulator’s product information (US FDA, EMA, ANSM, MHRA), a systematic review or a large registry study, and the details quote it word for word; two readers checked each one against its source. Where no such source states a fact, the entry leaves it out rather than guess. 9 of these medicines were on none of the lists above and enter the register on the experts’ list.
    • A second authority: 205 entries also carry a classification made by the Japanese government, through the GHS classification projects of the National Institute of Technology and Evaluation, to implement the labelling and safety-data-sheet duties of the Industrial Safety and Health Act and the PRTR Law. 62 of them have no EU harmonised entry. Each classification names the ministry that made it and the fiscal year, and links to its own page with the studies it rests on. It corroborates an entry and adds a jurisdiction; it does not set the level, because a GHS hazard code does not separate category 1A from category 1B.
    • What was decided, and by whom: the paragraphs below describe what the law provides. A tag on a card describes what an authority actually decided about that substance, and names it. 334 entries carry at least one. The European Commission has refused 92 of them as pesticide active substances and approved 26; 190 may not be sold to the general public at all; 256 may not go into a cosmetic product; 24 need a Commission authorisation for any use; 7 are eliminated worldwide by treaty; and 27 have been banned or severely restricted by at least one country, which the card names. An approval is not a contradiction: the EU excludes a category 1A or 1B reproductive toxicant from pesticide approval unless exposure is negligible, and category 2 is not excluded at all. It is, though, a decision worth seeing next to the evidence.
    • How much is tolerable: for substances in the food chain, the European Food Safety Authority derives the intake it considers tolerable and names the effect that figure rests on. 82 entries carry such a value, read from EFSA’s pages and from its chemical hazards database, OpenFoodTox 3.0 (CC BY-ND 4.0). EFSA does not classify teratogens and its remit stops at food and feed, so its values sit beside the evidence level, never instead of it.

    Independent hazard frameworks. Manufacturers and certifiers increasingly rate chemicals with two non-profit frameworks that score developmental and reproductive toxicity among their endpoints. GreenScreen for Safer Chemicals (Clean Production Action) assigns Benchmarks 1 to 4, Benchmark 1 being a chemical of high concern, through assessments by licensed profilers such as ToxServices; its free List Translator flags as LT-1 any chemical that an authoritative list already classes as a high-hazard reproductive or developmental toxicant, and its public registry tells you, by CAS number, whether a full assessment exists. ChemFORWARD rates chemicals used in consumer products and building materials in hazard bands A to F across 24 endpoints; its published rating guidance places in band F, by list screening alone, every substance with an EU harmonised Repr. 1 classification or on the REACH candidate and authorisation lists as a reproductive toxicant. The two organisations reported in 2021 that their outputs are aligned. Full assessments sit behind subscriptions, so this register cannot import their scores; where an entry meets ChemFORWARD's published F-band criterion, it says so.

    The EU harmonised classification is binding law: once a substance carries a hazard statement for the unborn child (H360D, H361d and their variants), every container of it, and of mixtures containing it, must be labelled across the EU and EEA; categories 1A and 1B may not be sold to the general public, cannot be approved as pesticides and are banned from cosmetics. It says nothing about finished articles, food or medicines, which are outside its scope. Proposition 65 is binding in California only and requires a warning before exposure, not a ban; it is enforced through litigation. EMA decisions bind marketing authorisations across the EU: the medicine stays available, under a pregnancy prevention programme. WHO guidance binds no one. Our bibliography reports evidence, not law.

Decisions come from four more public registers, read 2026-09-15: the EU Pesticides Database of DG SANTE, REACH Annex XIV and Annex XVII entry 30 as consolidated in Regulation 1907/2006, Annexes II and III of the cosmetics Regulation 1223/2009, the Stockholm Convention’s annexes, and the national bans and severe restrictions notified to the Rotterdam Convention. The two conventions publish names rather than identifiers, so those two are matched on name; the EU sources are matched on CAS number. The Rotterdam and Stockholm listings are assembled in the browser rather than served as data, so they are captured rather than fetched, and the capture is dated in the data file. 205 entries also carry the Japanese government’s own GHS classification, made by the National Institute of Technology and Evaluation for the ministries, 62 of them with no EU harmonised entry; ECHA’s site refuses automated requests, so those are read through PubChem, which republishes them, and matched on CAS number alone. Read 2026-09-14. The register takes assessments made by public authorities: the self-classifications companies notify for their own products are deliberately not used.
