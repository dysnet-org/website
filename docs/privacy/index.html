    Privacy · Articles 13 and 14 GDPR

    We collect nothing unless you say yes.

    This site has no advertising, no tracker that follows you elsewhere, no account and no form. The one thing it asks is whether we may count your visit, and a banner asks you once. Say no and nothing changes: every page and every register stays open either way. The registry runs as its own app, at registry.dysnet.org, and asks for each of its consents separately; section 05 is its notice. This page says what is processed, what our registers name, and how to exercise your rights. Questions go to info@dysnet.org.

    Version of 10 November 2026; earlier versions stay in the public history of this site. The controller is DysNet Ideell Förening, organisation number 802444-3015, Nybodagatan 1, 171 42 Solna, Sweden, with an office at Rue du Chantier 2, B-1000 Brussels. DysNet’s data protection officer is Tobias Arndt, at tobias.arndt@dysnet.org. Health Data Safe’s officer answers for your Health Data Safe account at dpo.contact@healthdatasafe.org.

    01 · The site

    What happens when you read a page.

    • Your request reaches our host, not us. GitHub Pages serves these files, so GitHub receives what every web request carries: your IP address, the time, the page and your browser. It uses that to deliver the page and to protect the service. We keep no visitor database and receive nothing visitor-level, so nobody here can look up who read what. Legal basis: our legitimate interest in a website that works, Article 6(1)(f). The site is static files with no database and no login, served over HTTPS.
    • We count visits only if you accept. A banner asks once. Accept, and Google Analytics records the page, the time, the country your address places you in, and your browser and device, and it gives your browser a random number so that a second page counts as the same visit rather than a new stranger. It tells us which registers people actually reach. It does not tell us who you are, and every advertising feature Google offers is switched off. Decline, and none of it loads and nothing is stored. Legal basis: your consent, Article 6(1)(a).
    • Your answer is remembered, and so is the map card. Your browser keeps your yes or no so the banner does not ask again, and on the pages with a map it keeps the word open or closed so the information card stays as you left it. Neither carries an identifier and neither reaches us. To change your answer, use the button in section 04.
    • Videos wait for you. Nothing loads from Google until you press play. Then Google receives your IP address and device information under its own policy.
    • The home map asks the registry for its dots. On the home page, your browser fetches the registry’s public map from registry.dysnet.org, our own server at Gandi in France. That server receives what every web request carries (your IP address, the time and your browser) and keeps it only in its rolling technical logs. What it sends back names nobody: only the areas and the words that people in the registry chose to show. Legal basis: our legitimate interest in showing the registry on our map, Article 6(1)(f).

    02 · Email

    If you write to us.

    Our mail runs on Zoho's European service, with Zoho Corporation B.V. in Utrecht as our processor. We read your message and answer it, and we keep the thread. We do not delete correspondence on a timer, because a question from a family or an association often resumes years later and that record is part of what the network is for. You can ask us to erase your correspondence at any time, and we will. Membership and donation records that count as accounting information stay for the seven years after the end of the calendar year in which the financial year closed, which Swedish law requires (Bokföringslag 1999:1078, chapter 7).

    Legal bases: answering the people who write to us, Article 6(1)(f); membership, donations and agreements, Article 6(1)(b) and (c). If you describe your own or your child's condition, you are sending health data. We may hold it because we are a non-profit body with a health aim, acting for our members and the people in regular contact with us, and because we disclose it to nobody outside (Article 9(2)(d)). Write in general terms if you would rather. We ask for no medical detail we do not need.

    03 · Registers

    The registers name professionals, not patients.

    Some of what we publish concerns named professionals we never asked, and Article 14 requires us to say so. The researcher register holds 204 research teams with their institution, country, the surnames and initials of first and last authors and their publications on our conditions, taken from the affiliations recorded in PubMed. The bibliography holds 1,934 references with their authors as published. The studies page carries the contact each study gives for itself, member associations the addresses they publish for themselves, and People our board and the volunteers who maintain the registers. Care centres and registries name institutions only.

    Legal basis: our legitimate interest, Article 6(1)(f), in publishing a free resource on conditions too few people study. We keep to professional information its holders have already published in a professional capacity, and we publish nothing about anyone's health. To be corrected or removed, write and name the entry: we act within 30 days and ask you for no reason. One limit we state openly, because the registers are open data under CC BY 4.0: a copy downloaded before a removal stays with whoever took it.

    04 · No wall

    The registers stay open.

    We considered putting the registers behind an analytics tracker you would have to accept. We had the question tested first, and it cannot be done lawfully. It would also be wrong: these pages describe a health condition, so a record that you read them would say something about your own or your child's health. A family looking up a diagnosis at two in the morning owes us nothing in exchange.

    This page used to promise that if we ever measured our traffic we would count pages rather than people, and say so here before we started. We are saying so. Since 22 September 2026 a banner asks whether we may count visits with Google Analytics, and it counts only for readers who accept. Refusing costs you nothing at all, which is the whole point: the registers are not the payment.

    You can change your mind whenever you like, in either direction.

    Why the wall is unlawful, for the reader who wants it: Swedish law allows an identifier to be stored on your device only with your consent, or where that is strictly necessary for the service you asked for (9 kap. 28 § lagen (2022:482) om elektronisk kommunikation). Consent extracted by withholding the content is not freely given (European Data Protection Board guidelines on consent of 4 May 2020, paragraphs 39 to 41). Our banner asks for that consent and takes no for an answer, leaving every page readable either way, which is the condition those guidelines set.

    05 · The registry

    What the registry reads, and who answers for it.

    The registry has been open since 3 November 2026, at registry.dysnet.org. This section is its privacy notice. It follows the roles that Health Data Safe sets out in its public compliance matrix, which describes how its accounts stand against the GDPR and Swiss data protection law.

    • Two controllers, each for its own part. Health Data Safe, a Swiss non-profit foundation in Morges, is the controller of your account: it runs the account, you hold it, and you decide who may read it, under its own privacy policy. We are the controller of what we read from your account with your permission, and of what the registry’s server keeps. Health Data Safe is not our processor: it acts on your permissions, never on our instructions, and we cannot ask it to open, change or delete your account.
    • What we read. Your agreement to be counted lets us read the condition, chosen from the Orphanet list or written in your own words; the date of birth, or the expected date before a birth; your answer to each consent; the identifier of the account they come from; and, if you give them, the place where the mother lived at the start of the pregnancy and what you choose to show on the registry’s map. We never see your password, and we read nothing else in your account. These are health data. If you agree to be contacted about research, we also read the email address of your Health Data Safe account.
    • Why, and on what basis. We read these data to count the people who live with dysmelia and to look for clusters of cases that research can investigate. We use your address only to ask you to sign back in, or to send you a new request such as a study asking for your consent, and we put a study to families only when a researcher has asked for it, the study has regulatory approval and DysNet’s Chair has agreed to it. Researchers never receive your address. Each purpose rests on your explicit consent (Article 9(2)(a) GDPR; Article 6(7) of the Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection). Each consent is a separate decision, and you can withdraw any of them at any time. For a child, a parent or guardian gives the consents in an account in the child’s name and holds that account until the child turns 18. Before birth, only the mother can register the child.
    • Where the data are, and who else handles them. Your answers stay in your Health Data Safe account, which Health Data Safe hosts in Switzerland. The registry app runs on a server at Gandi in France. Gandi acts as our processor, and the server keeps technical logs (your IP address, the time and the request) to run the service and protect it, which is our legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f)). To display the questions, your browser also fetches public files from Health Data Safe and from GitHub, which see your IP address as they would for any web page. When you place the pregnancy point on a map, your browser also reaches two OpenStreetMap services, as explained below. The app keeps you signed in with two cookies on your device, which last up to a year and are deleted when you sign out. It uses no analytics and loads no advertising.
    • Who sees what. Our registry team alone reads your record. Member associations do not see records. A researcher receives nothing unless you say yes to their study, and then only the data that study names, for that study alone. We sell nothing, we give nothing to insurers or employers, and we offer nobody bulk access.
    • If you ask to be invited. If you ask us for an invitation to the registry, we use your email address to send it, because you asked (Article 6(1)(f)). The registry keeps how many invitations it sent, not your address; your request itself stays with our mail, as section 02 says. Member associations never give us their members’ addresses: an association that wants its members to join forwards our invitation itself.
    • How long. We read your answers for as long as your permission lasts. Withdraw it in your Health Data Safe account and we can read nothing more; your answers stay in your account until you delete them yourself. We stop using your address the day you withdraw the research consent. Your account keeps a dated record of each consent you give. The server keeps its logs in rolling files of limited size, so the oldest entries are deleted as new ones arrive.

    Health Data Safe’s statutes rule out any sale of health data and limit its use to care and research, and they provide that personal data is never treated as an asset of the foundation.

    The place of the pregnancy, and the public map

    The registry asks for this place and offers the map, as described below.

    • The place of the pregnancy stays optional. The registry asks where the mother lived at the start of the pregnancy, as precisely as you can give it: an address, a click on a map, or coordinates. We read that point to look for clusters of cases. Only our registry team sees it, and it is never published.
    • Placing the point reaches OpenStreetMap. The map on which you place the point comes from OpenStreetMap’s tile server. Your browser asks it for the parts of the map you look at, so it receives your IP address, the address of the registry and roughly the area you are looking at. If you type an address, OpenStreetMap’s search service receives it together with your IP address, to find the point; only the point is saved in your account, never the address. The OpenStreetMap Foundation runs both services under its own privacy policy, not on our instructions.
    • Appearing on the map is a separate choice. It has its own consent: “I agree to appear on the public DysNet map”, or, for a child, “I agree that my child appears on the public DysNet map”. Saying no changes nothing about being in the registry, and the place of the pregnancy stays unpublished either way.
    • You choose two things before you agree. First, how widely your point is blurred: from 1 to 5 km, or wider. A circle shows you the area that would appear, centred away from your exact point. Second, the title of your dot, which is what the map says about you: it names your condition, it says roughly which limbs, or it says only that you live with a limb difference. A condition written in your own words always gets the last of these. You see the exact words before you agree.
    • The map shows a dot, never a place. The dot sits in the widened area, never at your exact point, and carries the title you chose and the number of people who share that area and that title. It never shows a name, an address or the exact place. The service that draws the map receives the dot alone: not your point, and not your condition unless you chose the title that names it.
    • Your dot appears because you agree. Your consent is the only condition: your dot appears at the next update of the map, even if nobody else shares your area, and it then stands for you alone and says “1 person”. Someone who knows you, or knows the area well, may then recognise you. The wider the area and the vaguer the title you choose, the harder that becomes, so choose both with that in mind.
    • What you show becomes public. Anyone can see the map: on the registry’s own page, on our home page, and in an open file that anyone may read and reuse. If you choose the title that names your condition, the map makes that health information public, attached to an area.
    • You can leave the map at any time. Use “Take me off the map” in the registry app, or withdraw the map access in your Health Data Safe account. The map is republished every 15 minutes, so your dot disappears at the next update rather than at once, and our home page may show it for up to five more minutes while browsers refresh their copy. Leaving does not undo what was lawful before you left (Article 7(3) GDPR). A copy that someone made of the map or of its file before you left stays with whoever made it, and we cannot recall it.
    • Both rest on your explicit consent. The place rests on your agreement to be counted, the map on its own consent (Article 9(2)(a) GDPR; Article 6(7) of the Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection). For a child, a parent or guardian decides, in the child’s account, until the child turns 18.

    06 · Your rights

    What you can ask, and whom to tell.

    You may ask us to do any of this, and Article 15 to Article 21 GDPR give you the right:

    • give you a copy of what we hold about you, or correct it;
    • erase it, or stop using it while a question about it is open;
    • hand it over in a form you can take elsewhere;
    • stop processing that rests on our legitimate interest.

    In the registry, you do not have to ask us. These rights are part of how the registry is built rather than a procedure wrapped around it. From your Health Data Safe account you review, correct, export and delete your data, and withdraw a consent, without writing to anybody; the registry app also gives you a copy of everything you entered in it. Health Data Safe publishes a self-service download of everything in an account and a route to delete it. You may still write to us, and we will carry out your request by hand.

    Consent, where we rely on it, can be withdrawn at any time. Write to info@dysnet.org. We answer within one month, free of charge, and if a request is genuinely complex we say so inside that month (Article 12(3)). We take no automated decisions about anybody and we build no profiles.

    Four companies process data for us: GitHub hosts this site from the United States, and states that it complies with the EU-US Data Privacy Framework; Zoho carries our mail on its European service; Google counts the visits of readers who accept the banner, from the United States, and states that it complies with the same framework; and Gandi runs the registry’s server in France. Nobody else. Health Data Safe is not on this list, because it holds your account as a controller in its own right, under its own policy. We use no advertising network, no data broker and no mailing-list service, and we have never sold or rented personal data.

    If we get something wrong, tell us, because most of it we can simply fix. You may also complain to a supervisory authority, choosing the one where you live, where you work or where you think the problem happened (Article 77), and you may go to court (Article 79). Ours is Integritetsskyddsmyndigheten, the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection: Box 8114, 104 20 Stockholm, imy@imy.se, +46 8 657 61 00, which takes complaints through its own form. In Switzerland, you may turn to the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner.
